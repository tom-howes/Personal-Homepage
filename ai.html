The science of bouldering grades

What even is a grade?

A bouldering grade is an attempt by one human to communicate to another human how hard a sequence of movements on rock (or plastic) is likely to feel. This sounds simple. It is not simple. Grades are subjective assessments made by individuals with different body types, different strengths, different levels of experience, and different ideas about what constitutes a fair challenge. The miracle is not that grades are inconsistent — it is that they are as consistent as they are.

The most widely used grading system in North America is the Hueco or V-scale, named after Hueco Tanks in Texas, where it was developed by climber John Sherman in the late 1980s. The scale runs from VB (beginner) through V0, V1, V2, and upwards, with the current confirmed upper end sitting around V17. Each grade represents a rough consensus about the difficulty of a set of movements — the holds, the angles, the distance between them, and the body positions required to link them together.

Why grades feel inconsistent

If you've climbed at more than one gym, you've probably noticed that a V4 at one gym feels nothing like a V4 at another. This is not your imagination. Gym grades are set by route setters — often talented climbers, but humans with their own movement preferences and physical attributes. A route setter who is tall and powerful might set a V4 that relies on long reaches and strong pulls. A smaller, more technical setter might create a V4 that rewards precise footwork and body positioning. Both might be genuinely V4 for the average climber at that gym, but they feel completely different.

Outdoor grades add another layer of complexity. Outdoor problems are graded by whoever first climbed them — the first ascentionist — and that grade travels with the problem through its history. A problem graded V5 in 1995 by a climber using the shoes and techniques of that era might be re-evaluated today as V4 or V6 depending on how techniques and equipment have evolved. Grades on outdoor rock also depend heavily on conditions: a problem at the top of its grade on a hot humid day might feel significantly easier when the temperature drops and the rock becomes more friction-friendly.

What actually makes a problem harder

Difficulty in bouldering comes from several interacting factors, none of which maps cleanly onto a single number:

  • Hold size and shape. Smaller holds require more finger strength and more precise placement. Slopers — rounded holds with no edge — demand friction and body position rather than grip strength. Crimps concentrate force on small sections of the fingertip and are both the most common source of injury and the most satisfying hold to stick.
  • Body position and movement. Some problems require specific sequences — a heel hook here, a drop knee there — that must be discovered and executed precisely. These technical problems can be disproportionately hard for climbers who haven't encountered the movement before, and disproportionately easy for those who have.
  • Physical demands. Power, contact strength, flexibility, and height all influence how hard a problem feels for a given climber. A dynamic move (dyno) over two metres rewards explosive power and timing. A high step rewards hip flexibility. A compression problem rewards the ability to squeeze two surfaces together with the whole body — a technique that tends to favour taller climbers.
  • Sequences and beta. Most problems have an intended sequence, but climbers frequently find alternative beta — different ways of doing the same movements — that suit their body better. This is part of why grades feel personal: the official grade assumes a particular approach, but your approach might be harder or easier.

Why climbers are obsessed with them

Grades provide a shared language and a measurable axis of progress in a sport where progress can otherwise be hard to see. There is genuine satisfaction in sending your first V4, then your first V5, then watching V4s that once felt impossible become warm-ups. The grade is a marker — imperfect, contested, sometimes arbitrary — but it is a marker.

There is also a social dimension. Grades are currency in climbing gyms. Saying you're "projecting a V6" communicates something to other climbers immediately — about where you are in your development, what kind of problems interest you, what you might be able to help each other with. The community that forms around shared projects and shared grades is part of what makes climbing gyms feel different from other fitness environments.

Perhaps most importantly: grades give you something to argue about. No climbing conversation is complete without someone suggesting that a particular problem is sandbagged (graded too easy) or soft (graded too hard). This is, depending on your perspective, either the most annoying or most endearing feature of climbing culture.

A personal note

I've been bouldering for about two years, primarily at Rockspot in Boston. I'm working on V5s, which mostly means getting shut down on V5s and occasionally fooling myself into thinking I nearly had it. The grade system frustrates me — some V4s feel like V6s, some V5s feel like V3s — but I also find it genuinely motivating. There's something satisfying about a system that is simultaneously objective (it's a number) and completely subjective (it's a number one person gave it one day based on how they felt).

If you climb and you're in Boston, come find me at Rockspot. I'll be the one staring at the same hold for too long.
